How to Fact-Check AI Trivia So You Never Read a Wrong Answer Aloud

8/15/2026

0 Comments

 
Short answer: AI-generated trivia is usually accurate, but it will occasionally state a wrong date, name, or statistic with complete confidence — and you can't tell a made-up fact from a real one just by reading it. The reliable fix is a five-minute spot-check before every show: verify anything with a specific number, date, superlative ("first," "only," "largest"), or recent event against one trusted source, and rewrite or cut anything you can't confirm. You don't need to check every question — you need to check the risky ones.
Reading one wrong answer aloud and losing an argument to a team that knows better costs you the room's trust for the whole night. Here's how to catch those before they happen, without turning prep into a research project.

Why AI gets facts wrong (and why it sounds so sure)

A language model predicts plausible text; it isn't looking anything up. Most of the time the most plausible answer is also the correct one, which is why AI trivia is right far more often than not. But when the true fact is obscure, contested, or very recent, the model can generate something that sounds exactly like a fact and simply isn't. It has no built-in signal for "I'm not sure about this one" — the wrong answers read with the same confidence as the right ones. That's the whole danger: the failure is invisible on the page.
A well-built generator reduces how often this happens — the Trivia Show Maker, for example, generates at a low "temperature" and instructs the model to skip anything it isn't confident about rather than guess (more on the mechanics in why AI trivia questions repeat and how to fix it). That cuts the error rate, but it doesn't drop it to zero. A quick human pass is still the last line of defense, and it's worth the five minutes.

Which questions to actually check

You don't have time to verify 50 questions, and you don't need to. Risk clusters around specific features. Spot-check any question that has one of these:
  • A specific number or statistic — heights, distances, populations, box-office totals, records. Numbers are where hallucinations hide, because a plausible-sounding wrong number looks identical to the right one.
  • A precise date or year — "in what year did X happen." Off-by-one and off-by-a-decade errors are common.
  • A superlative — "first," "only," "largest," "oldest," "fastest." These are frequently almost true (there's an earlier or bigger example the model forgot), which makes them prime dispute bait.
  • Anything recent — events from the last year or two. The model's knowledge has a cutoff, and it's least reliable near the edge of it.
  • Named attributions — "who wrote/invented/directed X." Easy to misattribute confidently.
Questions about broad, well-known facts ("what color is the sky at noon") almost never need checking. Concentrate your five minutes on the list above.

The five-minute fact-check workflow

  1. Scan for the red flags above and mark the 8-12 questions that hit one. Those are your check list.
  2. Verify each against one trusted source. A quick search that surfaces a reliable reference is enough for most; for anything contested, use two independent sources that agree.
  3. Watch the answer, not just the question. The question can be fine while the answer is subtly wrong — a right name with the wrong year, or a correct fact with an inflated number.
  4. Rewrite or cut, don't gamble. If you can't confirm it in under a minute, replace it. You have plenty of good questions; you don't need the shaky one.
  5. Keep a "known-good" bank. As you verify questions, save the winners. Over a few weeks you build a reusable set of pre-checked material and your per-show fact-checking shrinks.

Protect yourself live, too

Even with a clean set, be ready for a challenge:
  • Have your source handy for anything you suspect a team might contest, so you can settle it fast.
  • Have a graceful out. If a question is genuinely ambiguous or a team makes a fair case, award the point and move on. Being fair earns more trust than being right.
  • Keep a couple of pre-checked spares so you can swap out a question you lose confidence in mid-show.

The bottom line

AI makes writing trivia dramatically faster, and a good tool removes most of the accuracy risk before you ever see the questions. But "most" isn't "all," and the wrong answers are invisible until someone in the crowd catches them. A focused five-minute pass — checking only the questions with numbers, dates, superlatives, or recent events — is the difference between a host who sounds authoritative and one who gets argued into a corner.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if AI trivia is accurate?

Spot-check the risky questions against one trusted source before the show — anything with a specific number, a precise date, a superlative like "first" or "largest," a recent event, or a named attribution. Broad, well-known facts almost never need checking; the errors hide in the specifics.

Which AI trivia questions are most likely to be wrong?

Questions with specific statistics, exact years, superlatives ("first," "only," "largest"), events from the last year or two, and "who invented/wrote/directed" attributions. A plausible-sounding wrong number looks identical to a right one, which is exactly why those need a second look.

Does the Trivia Show Maker fact-check questions for me?

It reduces the error rate — it generates at a low temperature, tells the model to skip anything it isn't confident about, and drops malformed multiple-choice questions — but it doesn't replace a quick human pass. Give your final set a five-minute spot-check of the risky questions before hosting.

How long does it take to fact-check a trivia set?

About five minutes, because you only need to verify the risky questions — the ones with numbers, dates, superlatives, or recent events — not all 40 or 50. Rewrite or cut anything you can't confirm in under a minute.
